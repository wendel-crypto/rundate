Matches unlock three hours after you log a run.

Dating apps reward being on your phone. Training apps reward being outside. RunDate is built for people who would rather be outside: swipe mechanics on top of a real activity log, with a lock that makes the run the price of admission.

The read

Four observations that shaped the product.

01

Signal

Dating profiles optimise for photographs. Runners already generate better signal than a bio: pace, weekly mileage, personal records, the routes they actually run. The profile should be built out of training data.

02

Intent

A running app with dating bolted on attracts spectators. Gating matches behind a logged activity filters the pool down to people who actually run.

03

Geography

Running is local. Brickell is a 2.1 mile radius, not a city-wide pool. The geofence is quality control, not a limitation.

04

Proof

A photograph at a finish line beats a paragraph about loving the outdoors. The gallery and the activity feed carry the persuasion, so the interface gets out of their way.

The mechanic

The three-hour match lock.

This is the product. Everything else is a well-built dating app; the lock is the argument.

  • →

    The like button shows a padlock and stays disabled until three hours after the last logged activity.

  • →

    Swiping right before unlock raises a warning banner instead of producing a match.

  • →

    The Matches tab dims every contact and shows a live countdown card.

  • →

    The profile CTA reads “Unlock in Xh Xm” rather than Like.

  • →

    Log Activity states the rule before you save, and the confirmation screen repeats it.

It turns the app from a browsing habit into a reward for training, and it means everyone in the pool ran today.

The profile

Five tabs. One coherent person.

The profile is not a biography. It is a training dossier with enough human signal to start a conversation.

About

Bio, fun fact, Looking For, and a quick-info grid: preferred time, run days, next race, years running.

Stats

Personal records for 5K, half and full, physical stats and a weekly load chart. The signal a dating profile normally lacks.

Runs

The recent activity log, doubling as proof the person actually trains.

Gallery

A three-column photo grid that opens into a full-screen lightbox with a thumbnail strip.

Settings

Geofence radius and privacy sit at the top, because in this category they are product features, not admin.

Design decisions

Four choices that define the experience.

01

Image first. Every piece of information sits on the photograph over a gradient. No white cards, no blank space under a picture.

02

Match count removed from the profile. A visible total turns a dating app into a scoreboard and changes who feels welcome.

03

The geofence is shown, not hidden. “Brickell · 2.1 mi” sits in the header so the size of the pool is never a surprise.

04

Verification badges and online status ride on every card, because trust is the scarce thing in this category.

What I would measure

Six metrics. No vanity numbers.

Logged runs per active user per week

The lock only earns its place if it actually drives training.

Run to swipe conversion

Share of users who open Discover within an hour of unlock. Measures whether the delayed reward lands.

Match to first message

Whether shared training context makes the opening line easier to write.

Profile completion depth

Photos uploaded and tabs filled. The product is only as good as its profiles.

Geofence density

Active runners per square mile in Brickell. Below a threshold the pool feels empty and nothing else matters.

30-day retention

The honest read on whether the lock builds a habit or just adds friction.
